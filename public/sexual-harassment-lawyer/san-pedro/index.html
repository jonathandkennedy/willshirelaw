What You Need to Know

HR Protects the Company. We Protect You.

You've been told to "keep it professional," to "not make it a thing," to "let HR handle it." Here's what actually happens next.

"Investigations" that go nowhere

The company's investigation exists to create a paper record that they "took it seriously." It rarely exists to fix anything. Meanwhile, the harasser is still there — and so are you.

Retaliation is the second crime

Cut hours. A sudden bad review. A transfer. A layoff "for budget reasons" two months after you complained. Retaliation for reporting harassment is illegal on its own — and it usually makes the case bigger, not smaller.

You do not have to quit to have a case

Many people think they need to leave first. You don't. And if the environment became so intolerable you had no choice but to resign, the law treats that as being fired.

Straight Answers

Questions Everyone Asks

What counts as sexual harassment at work?

Two kinds. "Quid pro quo" — a boss or supervisor ties your job, raise, schedule, or promotion to sexual favors. And "hostile work environment" — unwanted comments, jokes, images, touching, or advances severe or frequent enough to make work miserable. It can come from a supervisor, a coworker, a customer, or a vendor. It does not have to be physical.

I never reported it. Do I still have a case?

Very possibly. Many people don't report because they're afraid — and the law understands that. Reporting can strengthen a case, but not reporting doesn't end it. Tell us what happened and we'll tell you honestly where you stand.

Will this be public?

Your consultation is completely confidential. Most cases settle privately, and settlements typically include confidentiality. We will never contact your employer until you decide you want to move forward.

How long do I have to file?

Deadlines are shorter than people think. Discrimination, harassment, and retaliation claims under California's Fair Employment and Housing Act must be filed with the Civil Rights Department within three years. Wrongful termination in violation of public policy generally carries a two-year deadline. Unpaid-wage claims reach back three years (up to four under the Unfair Competition Law). Some notice deadlines are far shorter — so get a free review now, not after the deadline passes.

I still work there. Can I really do this?

Yes. Retaliating against you for reporting harassment or contacting a lawyer is illegal and creates a second claim. We'll tell you exactly how to protect yourself — what to document, what to say, what not to sign — while your case moves forward.

What does it cost?

Nothing up front — ever. We front all costs, we only collect a fee if we recover money for you, and California law generally makes the employer pay your attorney's fees when you win.
